About the Author

Her journey with the mainframe began not in a lecture hall, but through the grit of self-directed research and a deep-seated obsession with how systems, both electrical and digital, hold up under pressure.

From early contributions to the openMosix cluster initiative and Slackware Linux, to managing zLinux environments at scale, Britley has always been drawn to Big Iron. To her, the mainframe was never a legacy burden, it was the ultimate engineering feat, a platform built for the kind of absolute reliability that mission-critical environments actually demand.

Britley lives in South Carolina, where she splits her time between architecting AI-native transaction systems, security research on the platforms everyone assumes are too old to matter, and running a fresh-foods hobby farm. She believes the mainframe will remain the governance anchor of the enterprise for the next sixty years, and that the people who understand its protocols at the byte level will be the ones who keep it trustworthy through whatever comes next.

A Closing Note

Silverlake started from a simple problem: the people who most need to understand an IBM i are the people least likely to get supervised time on one. The machine in the data center runs payroll. The machine at a hosting provider belongs to someone else. So the ones who defend these systems learn on the job, and the ones who audit them learn from a checklist written by someone who also never got to touch one.

A simulator will never be the real thing, and this book has tried to say so wherever it matters. What it can be is a place where the questions are the right ones. Who can sign on, and with what? What does this profile hold that it does not need? Which of these objects can I see but not use, and why does that gap exist? Which program is borrowing somebody else's authority, and does anyone check what it is handed? Those questions travel. They work on the system you are allowed to look at tomorrow, and they will still work the next time a vendor renames the product.

If you finished all five sessions, you have mapped what an ordinary profile can and cannot do, worked through the weak points a real review keeps finding, shut each one, and written down where it stopped working. That is not a complete IBM i education. Nobody gets one of those in five sessions. It is a real foundation, built on screens that actually appeared, and it is the habit of reading what the system tells you word for word.

The range keeps growing. There are lessons beyond this book, on the network services an IBM i exposes and on the small Unix shell inside it, and they are written up with the rest of the project. If you find a screen that does not look like the one on your system, a message that reads differently, or a lesson that should exist and does not, that is worth writing down and sending in. You know IBM i behaviour I have not seen, and the range gets better every time someone says so.
